01 · Abstract

What this paper asks

Between roughly 2015 and 2022, children grew up alongside search engines, screens and smartphones. Since late 2022, they have grown up alongside something categorically different: a machine that writes, reasons and answers for them. This dossier separates the two eras — because the evidence on each is different in kind.

We first establish the pre-AI baseline: what a decade of large surveys and longitudinal cohorts tells us about children's creative thinking, attention and memory before conversational AI existed. We then examine the AI era (2022–2026): the first wave of experiments measuring what happens when children and students hand cognition to a chatbot. We weigh the disadvantages and risks — cognitive, educational, social and safety-related — before a conclusion that states plainly what this evidence does and does not show.

Scope note — a global, not American, question

"Children" here spans roughly ages 3–19 (kindergarten through college entry). This is deliberately a global dossier: the baseline draws on UNESCO's Global Education Monitoring data, OECD assessments across 40+ systems, UK Ofcom and National Literacy Trust surveys, India's ASER household survey, and multinational neuroscience. The AI-era evidence is still concentrated in higher-income countries — itself one of the findings, because 1.3 billion of the world's school-age children have no home internet at all and so face this technology from the wrong side of a divide [R53].

02 · Method

How the evidence was gathered

Every statistic on this site was traced to a primary source — a peer-reviewed journal, a preprint with DOI, a government statistical agency, or a named research organization's published survey. No figure is presented without a working link on the References page.

Peer-reviewed experiments

Randomized and field experiments (MIT Media Lab EEG study; Wharton/PNAS Turkey field trial) measuring causal effects of LLM use on cognition and learning.

Longitudinal cohorts

Multi-year datasets: the NIH ABCD Study of 11,875 children aged 9–10, OECD PISA across ~80 education systems, McGill's 3-year GPS/spatial-memory follow-up, and India's ASER household survey of 650,000+ children.

National surveys

Pew Research Center teen surveys (2023, 2024, 2025), Common Sense Media census data, and Ofcom's UK Children and Parents Media Use surveys.

Global policy bodies

UNESCO's Global Education Monitoring Report and GenAI guidance, UNICEF–ITU connectivity data, and the Internet Watch Foundation's abuse-image analytics — the institutional layer that tracks every child, not just surveyed ones.

Part I · The Pre-AI Baseline (≈2015–2022)

The mind of the child before ChatGPT

I.1 Creativity: a measurable, decade-long slide

The strongest pre-AI signal comes from the Torrance Tests of Creative Thinking, the most widely used creativity assessment, with normative data stretching back to the 1960s. Kyung Hee Kim's landmark analysis of ~300,000 Torrance scores found that after rising for decades, American children's creative thinking scores began a significant decline from 1990 onward — steepest in kindergarten through 3rd grade — even as IQ scores continued to rise [R1]. William & Mary's coverage of the finding summarized it bluntly: "Smart? Yes. Creative? Not so much" [R2].

Importantly, the decline continued into the AI era. A 2025 systematic review in Thinking Skills and Creativity synthesized 45 studies and thousands of children's TTCT observations and concluded that overall creative potential declined by 0.6 SD across 2009–2025, with visual originality (the purest creative measure) falling 1.3 SD among kindergartners — one of the largest effect sizes in modern developmental psychology [R3].

I.2 Attention: shrinking before AI arrived

Long before chatbots, researchers were measuring attention fragmentation. UC Irvine's Gloria Mark tracked knowledge workers across two decades and found average focus on a single screen fell from 2½ minutes in 2004 to 75 seconds in 2012 to 47 seconds in recent years [R5]. The OECD's PISA 2022 — covering 15-year-olds in dozens of countries from Argentina to Japan — put numbers on the classroom version: 65% of students across OECD countries report being distracted by digital devices in at least some maths lessons, and those distracted by peers' devices score the equivalent of three-quarters of a year of learning lower in mathematics [R49]. The pattern is starkly global: over 50% of students in Argentina and Uruguay report classroom distraction versus 5% in Japan — the same technology, opposite outcomes, depending on policy [R50]. King's College London's 2022 survey adds the self-report layer: 49% of the UK public feels their attention span is shorter than it used to be [R4].

For children specifically, the screen-time literature is consistent: a 2025 scoping review in Children and Youth Services Review found a small but consistent correlation between screen use and concentration difficulties in children and young people [R6]. Longitudinal evidence is more alarming still: a Pediatrics analysis of the NIH ABCD cohort (2,947 children followed for 2 years) found each additional hour of daily screen time was linked to 12% higher odds of elevated ADHD-like symptoms at follow-up [R7]. And in the Global South the baseline starts lower: India's ASER 2024 household survey of 650,000+ children found ~90% of rural teens now have smartphone access at home, 82% can operate one — yet only 57% use it for study, while 76% use it for social media [R51]. Access without guidance is the default condition of childhood online, worldwide.

"Back in 2004, we found the average attention span on any screen to be two and a half minutes… we found it to average about 47 seconds." — Dr. Gloria Mark, UC Irvine · American Psychological Association

I.3 Memory: the Google effect, measured in children

The cognitive-science groundwork for AI dependence was laid by the "Google effect": people remember where information lives rather than the information itself [R8]. Kaspersky's 2015 surveys across Europe and the US put numbers on it: 79% of respondents said they now store what they consider important information on connected devices, and 91% admitted they use the internet as a memory extension rather than remembering things themselves [R9].

Navigation is the cleanest case study. McGill University researchers Dahmani & Bohbot showed that habitual GPS users have measurably worse spatial memory during self-guided navigation, and in a 3-year longitudinal follow-up, daily GPS-dependent navigation predicted decline in spatial memory ability itself [R10]. For a developing hippocampus, offloading wayfinding is not neutral — the capacity atrophies when unused.

I.4 Academic foundations: the decade's slow erosion

The decade before ChatGPT also saw the first sustained reversal of post-war academic progress. OECD PISA 2022 — the world's largest education assessment, covering 15-year-olds in ~80 systems — recorded a record 15-point drop in mathematics and 10 points in reading between 2018 and 2022, the largest decline in the assessment's history [R11]. The US NAEP long-term trend told the same story at home: 9-year-olds lost 5 points in reading and 7 in math between 2020 and 2022 — the largest declines since testing began in the 1970s [R12]. In India, ASER's long-run household data show foundational reading recovered only partially post-pandemic, with roughly a quarter of Std III children unable to read a Std II text in 2024 [R52]. A decade's lost progress, largely attributed to pandemic disruption layered on existing screen-driven fragmentation [R13].

Part II · The AI Era (2022–2026)

What changes when the machine thinks for you

II.1 Adoption: how fast children moved to AI

The adoption curve is the fastest in the history of education technology — and it is global. Pew Research Center found the share of US teens using ChatGPT for schoolwork doubled from 13% in 2023 to 26% in 2024 [R14]; by late 2025, 64% of US teens used AI chatbots at all, about 3 in 10 daily [R15]. The UK's National Literacy Trust surveyed over 80,000 children aged 8–17 and found 77% had already used generative AI in 2024 [R54]. India's ASER 2024 found ~90% of rural 14–16-year-olds have smartphone access at home — the entry point for a generation [R51]. OpenAI's own research puts weekly ChatGPT use among US college-aged adults at roughly one-third [R16]. And Common Sense Media found 7 in 10 teens had used at least one generative AI tool by 2024, with homework help the most common use [R17]. The direction is identical everywhere measured — only the speed differs.

II.2 "Cognitive debt": the first neural evidence

The single most cited experiment on AI and cognition is MIT Media Lab's "Your Brain on ChatGPT" (Kosmyna et al., 2025). Fifty-four participants wrote SAT-style essays across four sessions in three groups: brain-only, search engine, or LLM. EEG recordings showed a strict dose-response of brain connectivity: brain-only writers showed the strongest, most distributed neural networks; search users moderate; LLM users the weakest [R18].

The behavioral results were starker still. When asked to quote their own essays minutes after writing them, 83% of LLM users could not produce a single correct quote — versus 11% of brain-only writers. The LLM group reported the lowest sense of ownership over their own writing. In the fourth session, writers who had used ChatGPT and were switched to brain-only showed reduced alpha and beta connectivity — under-engagement that persisted. The authors coined the term "cognitive debt": the long-run cost of convenience now [R19].

"While LLMs offer immediate convenience, our findings highlight potential cognitive costs. Over four months, LLM users consistently underperformed at neural, linguistic, and behavioral levels." — Kosmyna et al. (2025) · arXiv:2506.08872

II.3 Learning outcomes: practice up, tests down

A large field experiment in a Turkish high school (Bastani et al., published in PNAS, 2025) gave ~1,000 math students GPT-4-based tutors. During practice, results looked spectacular: +48% more correct problems with a base GPT-4, +127% with a scaffolded "GPT Tutor". Then came the unassisted exam: students who had used the base ChatGPT scored 17% worse than peers who never had AI — the AI had functioned as a "crutch," and the scaffolded tutor, though it eliminated the harm, produced no lasting gain [R20]. Independent coverage in The Hechinger Report summarized it: kids who use ChatGPT as a study assistant do worse on tests [R21].

II.4 Critical thinking: the offloading correlation

Two large correlational studies converge on the same mechanism. Gerlich (2025, Societies) surveyed 666 UK participants across age groups and found a significant negative correlation between frequent AI-tool use and critical-thinking performance, mediated by cognitive offloading — with younger users (17–25) showing the highest dependence and lowest critical-thinking scores [R22]. Microsoft Research and Carnegie Mellon's survey of 319 knowledge workers and 936 real GenAI tasks found the same pattern at work: higher confidence in AI predicted less critical-thinking effort — users shift from execution to verification, and often not even that [R23]. A randomized experiment (Georgiou, 2025) then showed it causally in writing tasks: students assigned ChatGPT scored significantly lower on a validated cognitive-engagement scale — less mental effort, less deep processing, less strategic thinking — than unassisted peers [R24].

II.5 What AI-assisted learning can do

Honesty requires the other side of the ledger. A 2025 meta-analysis of 51 ChatGPT studies (Wang et al., JCAL) found a moderately positive overall effect on academic achievement (g = 0.577) when AI is integrated with sound pedagogy [R25]. OpenAI's "study mode" — launched July 2025 — builds deliberate friction (step-by-step guidance instead of answers) directly in response to the offloading literature [R26]. And the Wharton field trial showed a scaffolded tutor that withholds answers eliminates the test-score harm entirely. The variable is not the technology but the design of its use — a point UNESCO codified when it urged governments to regulate classroom GenAI and set a minimum age of 13 for classroom use [R27].

The design principle the evidence supports

AI that replaces retrieval and reasoning produces cognitive debt. AI that withholds, questions, and scaffolds — forcing the child to think first — preserves learning while keeping the benefits. Every positive result in this dossier comes from the second kind.

04 · Disadvantages & Risks

The costs — cognitive, educational, social, and safety

A research paper on children and AI that lists only benefits would be negligent; one that lists only harms would be dishonest. This section consolidates the disadvantages documented across the dossier — and adds the risks that are global, structural, and often left out of US-centric debates.

D.1 Cognitive disadvantages — the mind that stops practicing

  • Cognitive debt. MIT's EEG evidence shows AI-assisted writers develop weaker neural connectivity, remember less of their own work, and underperform even after they stop using the tool [R18].
  • Skill atrophy through offloading. The GPS literature proves the mechanism: capacities that are offloaded daily measurably decline [R10]. Critical thinking shows the same correlation in heavier AI users [R22].
  • Illusion of competence. Practice scores soar (+48–127%) while unassisted exam performance falls (−17%) — children can feel expert while learning less [R20].
  • Attention fragmentation. 65% of OECD students report digital distraction in maths lessons; peer distraction alone costs ~¾ of a year of learning progress [R49].

D.2 Educational disadvantages — the global picture

  • UNESCO's verdict. The 2023 GEM Report found a global evidence base "dominated by those with a vested interest" in selling edtech, with too little independent proof of benefit and real evidence of distraction, privacy exposure, and inequity [R47].
  • Policy whiplash worldwide. By end-2024, 40% of the world's education systems had banned smartphones in schools — rising to 114 systems (58% of countries) by 2026 — a worldwide institutional judgment that unmanaged screens harm learning [R48] [R55].
  • The Digital divide. 1.3 billion school-age children (two-thirds globally) have no home internet; in low-income countries fewer than 1 in 20 do. AI in education risks amplifying gaps rather than closing them [R53].
  • The Sweden warning. One of the world's most digitized school systems is now pouring money back into printed textbooks after a decade of screen-first schooling coincided with declining reading skills — a national reversal, not a parental opinion [R56].
  • Assessment integrity. UNESCO and OECD both flag that AI-assisted cheating threatens the validity of the assessments on which school systems are judged [R30].

D.3 Social & developmental disadvantages

  • Displacement of developmental activity. Creativity's documented slide correlates with the displacement of pretend play, unstructured exploration and independent mobility by screens [R3] [R44].
  • Reduced ownership & confidence. The MIT study found AI users felt the lowest ownership of their own essays — an identity-level cost for children still discovering what their own mind can do [R18].
  • Unequal access to guidance. India's ASER shows the modal pattern in the Global South: near-universal smartphone access, but only ~57% of that use for study and no adult mediation — technology without mentorship [R51].

D.4 Safety risks — the harm that is already industrialized

The gravest global disadvantage is criminal. The Internet Watch Foundation — which assesses child sexual abuse imagery worldwide — reports that generative AI is now systematically used to manufacture child sexual abuse material: 2025 was the worst year in the organization's 30-year history, with a "frightening" ~260-fold rise in photo-realistic AI videos of child sexual abuse and a 400% surge in AI-generated imagery reports during the year [R57]. This is the disadvantage that no educational policy can mitigate alone — it requires platform design, law enforcement, and regulation, which is why UNESCO's guidance insists on data-privacy protection and age limits as preconditions for classroom AI [R27].

The balance of harms, stated plainly

The disadvantages are real, documented, and global — but they are conditional. Every cognitive harm in this dossier is a function of unguided, substitutive use. The same tools, used with guardrails (withholding answers, age limits, teacher mediation, equity investment), show no measurable harm and some measurable benefit. The risk is not AI; it is AI instead of thinking.

05 · Honesty Section

What this evidence cannot yet tell us

A research paper that hides its weaknesses is marketing. These are the genuine limits of the current evidence — and of this dossier:

Correlation is not causation. The offloading findings (Gerlich; Microsoft/CMU) are cross-sectional: kids who already find thinking hard may reach for AI more, not only the reverse. The causal chain is supported but not settled.

Age mismatch. Most rigorous AI experiments are on older teens, college students and adults. Direct evidence on young children (3–10) and chatbots barely exists yet — extrapolation is inference, not data.

Small samples, short horizons. The MIT EEG study had 54 participants over four months; the PNAS trial one school. No study has followed a child's cognition across a full childhood of AI use.

Confounded decade. PISA's record decline and the creativity slide overlap with a pandemic, a smartphone epoch and social-media saturation. Isolating "AI" as a cause is currently impossible — and this paper does not attempt it.

Publication timing. The headline AI-cognition studies are mostly preprints or very recent (2025–2026); peer review and replication are still catching up. Treat magnitudes as provisional.

06 · Implications

What the evidence suggests for parents & educators

REC-01

Delay unsupervised chatbots

Follow UNESCO's guidance: minimum age 13 for classroom GenAI, later for unsupervised use — ChatGPT's own terms require 13+ with parental consent. The developing years build retrieval habits that offloading dissolves.

REC-02

Think first, AI second

The PNAS and MIT results converge: attempt the problem unaided before consulting a model. Tools like OpenAI's Study Mode formalize this "effort-first" order.

REC-03

Protect unstructured play

Creativity declines tracked screen saturation, not schooling. Independent exploration and pretend play remain the strongest documented inputs to divergent thinking in children.

REC-03b

Close the divide before deploying the tech

With 1.3 billion children offline at home, AI-in-education investment that ignores connectivity widens the very gaps it promises to close (UNICEF–ITU). Devices are not the bottleneck; electricity, broadband and teacher training are.

REC-04

Guard the attention budget

Screen-time and attention data (ABCD, Leonhardt et al.) point the same way: fragmenting media before homework degrades the deep-focus capacity that both learning and creativity require.

REC-05

Treat AI like GPS, not a chauffeur

Spatial-memory research shows skills fade when fully offloaded, persist when guided. The same rule of thumb for cognitive skills: use AI to check, extend and explain — not to substitute.

REC-06

Ask for the source, always

Teach children that any claim — including from this site — needs a link and a check. The References page models that habit; make children practice it too.

REC-07

Follow the world's regulatory direction

58% of countries now restrict phones in schools and UNESCO sets age-13 as a floor for classroom GenAI. National policy is converging on one lesson: unmanaged screens harm learning; governed ones need not.

07 · Conclusion

What this research conveys

This paper set out to answer one question across a decade and dozens of countries: what is happening to the minds of children — before AI, and after it?

C.1 The five findings

First, the decline predates AI. Children's creative thinking, attention and memory were already eroding through the smartphone decade (2015–2022): a measurable creativity slide across 45 studies, 47-second average screen attention, record PISA reversals. AI did not create the weakness — it inherited it.

Second, unguided AI deepens the weakness. In the first causal experiments on record, chatbot-assisted students showed the weakest brain connectivity, the poorest memory of their own work, a 17% exam deficit despite practice gains, and significantly lower cognitive engagement. The mechanism is cognitive offloading — the same one that let GPS erode spatial memory — now applied to thinking itself.

Third, the harm is conditional, not intrinsic. The same tools, scaffolded to withhold answers, show no harm and some benefit; the meta-analytic effect of pedagogically integrated AI is positive. The variable is never the model — it is whether the child thinks first or the machine thinks instead.

Fourth, the disadvantages are global and uneven. UNESCO's verdict on edtech evidence, phone bans spreading across 58% of the world's education systems, Sweden's national retreat to textbooks, and 1.3 billion children with no home internet together describe a world adopting AI for children faster than it can govern it — with the greatest risks concentrated where guidance and infrastructure are thinnest. And one harm is already criminal in scale: the industrial production of AI-generated child sexual abuse material, which no school policy can solve alone.

Fifth, the window is now. Every rigorous AI-cognition study in this dossier is five years old or less. The habits of a generation are being set before the evidence is settled. That asymmetry — adoption outrunning understanding — is the single most important fact this research conveys.

C.2 The bottom line

Children's minds were already softening under screens; AI is the first technology that can reason in place of a child, and the evidence says that handed over wholesale, it accumulates cognitive debt — but governed, scaffolded and equitably deployed, it remains a tool and not a replacement. The conclusion of this research is not a warning against machines. It is a warning against letting the machine do the growing up. — Conclusion of the dossier · every clause sourced in the References

For parents, the mandate is sequence: think first, AI second. For teachers, it is design: scaffold, don't substitute. For governments, it is the UNESCO floor — age limits, privacy, evidence before deployment. And for all of us, it is curiosity without surrender: the child's own mind remains the instrument this entire century depends on.
